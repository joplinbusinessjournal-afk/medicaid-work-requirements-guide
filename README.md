# Medicaid Work Requirements in 2026–2027: What You Actually Have to Do

If you're on Medicaid, you've probably heard that new work requirements are coming — and that the letters explaining them read like they were written by lawyers, for lawyers. This guide explains the rules the way a navigator would: plainly, and with what to do next.

## What is the Medicaid work requirement?

Starting January 1, 2027, federal law requires most adults in Medicaid's expansion group to complete 80 hours a month of work or related activities — a job, job training, education, volunteering, or a mix. Some states started earlier under their own programs. If you don't meet the requirement and don't qualify for an exemption, you can lose your coverage.

## Who does it apply to?

It applies to adults ages 19–64 in the ACA Medicaid expansion group who don't fall into an exempt category. It does not apply to children, pregnant women, people 65 and older, or people the state considers medically frail, among others.

## The 11 exemption categories (simplified)

You may be exempt if you are: 65 or older; blind or disabled; medically frail (a serious health condition — this is the category most people miss); a caregiver for a child or a person who can't care for themselves; pregnant or recently postpartum; a former foster youth under 26; in a drug or alcohol treatment program; a student or in job training; receiving unemployment or certain other benefits; or facing a short-term crisis that keeps you from working.

Each exemption has its own documentation rules, and they vary by state. An exemption you don't claim doesn't protect you — which is why checking takes five minutes and is worth doing before any deadline.

## How do you report your hours?

Most states require monthly reporting — usually through the state's benefits portal, by phone, or in person. The critical detail: reporting deadlines are strict. In early state rollouts, the overwhelming majority of coverage losses came from paperwork problems — missed notices, misunderstood forms, uploads the system rejected — not from people failing to work the hours.

Practical tips: report early in the month, not on the deadline day. Keep copies (screenshots or printouts) of everything you submit. If your address changes, update it with the Medicaid agency immediately — a notice sent to an old address still counts as notice.

## What if you lose coverage?

You have the right to appeal — every state has a fair-hearing process with real deadlines, usually measured in days from the notice date. Don't wait.

## State-by-state rules

Start dates, reporting portals, and accepted documentation differ by state. Each state guide includes the official state agency link so you can verify everything yourself.

## The one thing to remember

The work requirement won't be decided by whether you work. For most people, it will be decided by whether the paperwork is right and on time. Check your exemption status now, set a monthly reporting reminder, and keep copies of everything. Five minutes today can save your coverage tomorrow.
